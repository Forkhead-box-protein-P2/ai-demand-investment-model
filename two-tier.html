AI Demand, Compute Pricing, and Investment

Does the extra usage generated by faster commodification and lower prices under pacing outweigh the losses from slower capability growth and reduced frontier-training demand? This two-tier model compares two moving capability distributions: frontier and commodity values evolve differently at each pace, while ordinary adoption growth is shared. Its research-anchored baseline and unmeasured pacing assumptions are explained below.

Model

A minimal two-tier dynamic partial-equilibrium model from user choice to new compute investment.

final AI usage → compute demand → compute price & cash flow → NPV → investment → future capacity

The model asks: does the extra usage generated by faster commodification and lower prices under pacing outweigh the losses from slower capability growth and reduced frontier-training demand? It compares two moving capability distributions, represented by frontier and commodity value indices within each scenario. Full pace creates new frontier value faster; pacing makes the commodity tier catch up faster relative to a frontier that advances more slowly. Users choose frontier AI, commodity AI, or no AI based on each scenario’s values and prices. Their usage determines compute demand, cash flow, and the return on new capacity.

ChannelFull frontier pacePacing
Frontier capability/valueFaster frontier growthSlower frontier growth
Commodity capability/valueSlower relative catch-up toward a faster moving frontierFaster relative catch-up toward a slower moving frontier
Frontier markupMore persistent markupLarger markup erosion and a lower persistent floor
Frontier-training computeMore frontier-training demandLess frontier-training demand

These channels operate together when their strengths are positive. The model does not reuse the full-pace capability path at lower prices. Ordinary diffusion/adoption is shared; relative commodity catch-up is distinct from that adoption process.

Two services, two prices

The earlier single-price formulation put a frontier-influenced price on every unit of AI usage. It could not represent users choosing cheap legacy or open models while others paid for frontier capability. Here both services exist at every frontier pace, including full progress. Frontier and commodity usage are solved separately; a usage-weighted average price is only a descriptive output, never the price faced by all users.

\[P_{F,t}=c+\kappa_{F,t}p_{K,t}+\mu_{F,t}(g)+\mu_A,\qquad P_{C,t}=c+\kappa_{C,t}p_{K,t}+\mu_A.\]

\(P_F,P_C\) are service prices. \(c\) is shared non-compute cost; \(p_K\) is the common compute price; \(\kappa_F,\kappa_C\) are compute per use. Only the frontier tier earns the additive frontier markup \(\mu_F\). Both tiers retain the existing application markup \(\mu_A\). These markups are normalized price units, not measured gross-margin percentages.

\[\kappa_{F,t}=(1-e)^t,\qquad\kappa_{C,t}=\rho\kappa_{F,t},\qquad 0<\rho\leq1.\]

\(e\) is the common annual efficiency improvement and \(\rho\) is the commodity tier's relative compute intensity. This keeps the old efficiency channel while allowing cheaper models to use less compute per service.

Capability, progress, and catch-up

\(g\in[0,1]\) remains continuous frontier pace: 1 is full progress and 0 is a full pause. Frontier capability/value \(V_F\) grows at annual rate \(\phi g\). Commodity value \(V_C\) catches up toward its own scenario’s frontier at time \(t\), which keeps moving when \(\phi g>0\). The implemented closed form is exactly equivalent to the growth recurrence:

\[V_{F,t+1}=V_{F,t}(1+\phi g),\qquad V_{F,t}=V_{F,0}(1+\phi g)^t,\qquad V_{C,0}=1,\]
\[V_{C,t+1}=V_{C,t}+d(g)(V_{F,t}-V_{C,t}),\qquad d(g)=1-2^{-[1+a(1-g)]/h_C}.\]

\(V_{F,0}\) is the initial frontier value premium, \(\phi\) is annual frontier value growth at full pace, and \(h_C\) is the catch-up half-life at full pace. Commodity models improve even at \(g=1\). Their catch-up half-life is \(h_C/[1+a(1-g)]\). The baseline \(h_C=0.5\) year is a loose timing proxy, while \(a=1\) assumes a full pause halves the catch-up half-life. Set \(a=0\) to remove that additional pacing effect. The markup half-life \(h\) is independent: capability benchmarks do not measure profit-margin decay. For \(a>0\), \(d'(g)<0\): pacing speeds gap closure toward a more slowly advancing frontier. Faster relative catch-up does not guarantee higher absolute commodity capability than under full progress. At \(g=0\) the frontier stops growing; that is the full-pause limit, not the interpretation of every paced path. Setting \(\phi=0\) deliberately removes frontier growth from both scenarios.

The existing markup rule is preserved:

\[\mu_{F,t}(g)=\mu_{F,0}\left[g+(1-g)2^{-t/h}\right].\]

Full progress continually renews the frontier markup. Lower pace exposes a larger fraction of the initial markup to erosion and lowers its persistent floor \(g\mu_{F,0}\); a full pause erodes the entire markup. For \(t>0\) and positive initial markup, lower \(g\) means a lower markup, and its decline over time is larger. The transient component retains half-life \(h\): pace changes the erosion amount and persistence rather than that half-life. This is a reduced-form rule, not a strategic pricing game.

Users sort by price and value

A minimal multinomial logit gives each usage opportunity three choices: frontier AI, commodity AI, or no AI. The outside option has utility \(\ln\omega\), where \(\omega>0\) controls non-use relative to both AI services; the baseline sets \(\omega=1\). For tier \(j\in\{F,C\}\), systematic utility is \(u_j=\ln V_j-\beta\ln(P_j/\bar P)\). Standard logit shares are therefore

\[w_j=V_j(P_j/\bar P)^{-\beta},\quad s_F=\frac{w_F}{\omega+w_F+w_C},\quad s_C=\frac{w_C}{\omega+w_F+w_C},\quad s_0=\frac{\omega}{\omega+w_F+w_C}.\]

\(\beta\) is price sensitivity and \(\bar P\) is a fixed normalization price shared by both tiers and scenarios. Higher relative value or a lower relative price attracts users. Raising commodity value shifts demand from the frontier tier and from non-use into commodity AI, holding other values and prices fixed. That holding-other-values-fixed substitution effect is only one channel. Under pacing, frontier value also grows more slowly, commodity value follows that slower moving frontier, and the frontier markup compresses. Compression can attract users back to frontier service. Choice shares use the resulting values and prices together; the net tier mix is calculated, not forced.

\[M_t=M_0(1+g_D)^t,\qquad Q_{F,t}=M_ts_{F,t},\qquad Q_{C,t}=M_ts_{C,t},\qquad Q_t=Q_{F,t}+Q_{C,t}.\]

\(M_t\) is the market of potential usage opportunities, and \(g_D\) is ordinary diffusion/adoption growth, identical in both scenarios. Pacing receives no separate generic diffusion bonus. Faster relative capability catch-up is represented only in \(d(g)\), toward the paced frontier. Prices and capabilities also change participation \(s_F+s_C\). The frontier share among AI users is \(Q_F/(Q_F+Q_C)\), which differs from its share of all opportunities \(s_F\).

The old constant demand elasticity is replaced by logit price sensitivity. Tier \(j\)'s own-price elasticity is \(-\beta(1-s_j)\); a common proportional increase in both service prices gives total-usage elasticity \(-\beta s_0\). The researched baseline uses an own-service elasticity proxy to calibrate \(\beta\), not an aggregate elasticity. Its implied aggregate elasticity is displayed in the calibration checks below. \(\omega\) is varied independently because firm adoption rates do not identify the share of task opportunities served by AI. The logit choice structure assumes independent unobserved preferences and proportional substitution; its coefficients and tier values are not fitted to AI customer data. See the logit method reference below.

For each parameter draw, \(\bar P=c+p_{K,0}+\mu_{F,0}+\mu_A\), with \(p_{K,0}=0.35\). Compute initial reference shares at \(g=1\); set \(M_0=1/(s_{F,0}+s_{C,0})\), so initial reference usage is one. The same \(M_0\), \(\bar P\), initial capacity, and initial capabilities are then used in both scenarios. Commodity value updates after each annual equilibrium.

From usage to compute
\[K_{I,t}=\kappa_{F,t}Q_{F,t}+\kappa_{C,t}Q_{C,t},\qquad K_t^D=K_{I,t}+T_t(g),\qquad T_t(g)=gT_0.\]

\(K_I\) is inference compute, \(T\) is frontier-training compute, and \(K^D\) is total compute demand. With initial training share \(\tau\), set \(T_0=K_{I,0}\tau/(1-\tau)\) and \(S_0=K_{I,0}+T_0\). This preserves the meaning of the training-share control after inference splits into two tiers. Lower frontier pace reduces training demand directly through \(gT_0\). More inference usage can offset this loss, but total usage alone does not establish whether total compute or investment rises.

Compute-market equilibrium
\[p_{K,t}=v+(p_{K,0}-v)\left(\frac{K_t^D}{S_t}\right)^{1/\sigma}.\]

\(S_t\) is effective capacity, \(v=0.18\) is the variable-cost floor, and \(\sigma\) is short-run supply elasticity. Non-compute cost remains \(c=0.25\). Because both service prices depend on the compute price, each year solves the same compute-market fixed point:

\[p_K\longrightarrow(P_F,P_C)\longrightarrow(Q_F,Q_C)\longrightarrow K^D\longrightarrow p_K.\]

How the equilibrium is computed: hold capacity, capabilities, and scenario assumptions fixed; bracket a compute price above the cost floor; then bisect until the price implied by demand matches the price being tested. Both tiers' demand is recomputed at every step. This improves numerical convergence without changing the supply schedule.

Cash flow, NPV, and investment
\[H_t=p_{K,t}K_t^D,\qquad CF_t=(p_{K,t}-v)K_t^D,\qquad NPV_t=\frac{CF_t}{S_t}A(r,\operatorname{round}(L))-b.\]

\(H\) is compute-supplier revenue and \(CF\) is its operating cash flow. The annuity factor \(A\) discounts constant current per-capacity cash flow over the rounded economic life; \(L\) is the unrounded life control, \(r\) is the discount rate, and \(b\) is build cost per unit of capacity. Service markups are not added to compute-supplier revenue. This retains the existing NPV approximation rather than introducing a forecast of future prices.

\[\widehat\iota_t=\max\!\left(\delta+\alpha\frac{NPV_t}{b},0\right),\quad\iota_t=\tfrac12\iota_{t-1}+\tfrac12\widehat\iota_t,\quad I_t=\iota_tS_t,\]
\[S_{t+1}=(1-\delta)S_t+I_t,\qquad\delta=1/L,\qquad\iota_{-1}=\delta.\]

\(\alpha\) is investment response and \(\iota\) is the gradually adjusting investment rate. The capacity, cash-flow, NPV, depreciation, and investment rules are unchanged. Economic life determines the annuity's rounded number of years; depreciation uses the unrounded life control.

Timing and transition: time zero is when frontier pace changes. Both scenarios inherit the same capacity and initial investment rate. Training demand changes immediately to \(gT_0\), while installed capacity adjusts through investment and retirement. This can create an initial drop in returns and investment, followed by a recovery as capacity retires; gradual adjustment can overshoot. These annual paths are transition dynamics, not a prediction that investment must rise smoothly. Investment includes replacement of retiring capacity; net capacity addition is \(I_t-\delta S_t\).

For an \(n\)-year horizon, cumulative investment is \(\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}I_t\). The chart also shows the annual flow at \(t=n\), which starts the next year and is excluded from that cumulative total. Total usage, tier shares, and revenue comparisons use the endpoint \(t=n\). Ordinary adoption increases opportunities, while efficiency reduces compute per use: before changes in participation and tier mix, inference compute grows by \((1+g_D)(1-e)\) per year. For example, 25% adoption growth and 20% efficiency improvement give \(1.25\times0.8=1\); more usage need not imply growing compute investment.

Pacing can raise or lower compute investment. Faster relative catch-up and lower prices can increase participation, but this catch-up is toward a more slowly advancing frontier; it does not guarantee higher absolute commodity capability. Reduced frontier-training demand and shifts toward less compute-intensive service can also weaken total compute demand. Frontier markup compression may attract users back to the frontier tier. The net effect of all four pacing channels determines compute scarcity, supplier cash flow, NPV, and investment; no investment sign or final tier share is imposed.
