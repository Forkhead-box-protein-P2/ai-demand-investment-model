Model

A minimal dynamic partial-equilibrium model from final AI demand to new compute investment.

final AI usage → compute demand → compute price & cash flow → NPV → investment → future capacity

The model formalizes one question: if frontier progress slows, does the extra usage created by lower prices offset the loss of frontier-driven demand strongly enough to preserve the economics of new compute investment? It starts with final users, solves the compute market, and ends with the expected return on another unit of capacity.

Final demand and pricing

\(\boldsymbol{Q_t}\) is final AI usage in year \(t\), \(\boldsymbol{P_t}\) is the final price of the AI service, and \(\boldsymbol{Z_t}\) shifts demand outward as AI becomes more useful or more widely adopted. Final demand is

\[Q_t = Z_t(g)\left(\frac{P_t}{P_0}\right)^{-\varepsilon}.\]

\(\boldsymbol{P_0}\) is the initial normalization price and \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) is the price elasticity of final demand. A lower price raises usage; a larger \(Z_t\) shifts the whole demand curve outward.

The final service price is ordinary non-compute cost plus compute cost plus the markups supported by model and application differentiation:

\[P_t = c + \kappa_t p_{K,t} + \mu_{F,t}(g) + \mu_A.\]

\(\boldsymbol{c}\) is non-compute cost, \(\boldsymbol{\kappa_t}\) is compute required per unit of AI service, \(\boldsymbol{p_{K,t}}\) is the compute price, \(\boldsymbol{\mu_{F,t}}\) is the frontier-model markup, and \(\boldsymbol{\mu_A}\) is the application-layer markup. As frontier differentiation erodes, \(\mu_{F,t}\) falls, lowering the final service price and increasing usage.

Frontier progress also creates user value; it is not modeled as pure pricing power. \(\boldsymbol{g}\) is frontier pace, with \(g=1\) equal to the reference pace and \(g=0\) a full pause. \(\boldsymbol{g_D}\) is demand growth from diffusion and adoption that occurs at every frontier pace, while \(\boldsymbol{\phi}\) is the additional demand growth created by frontier capability progress:

\[Z_t(g)=Z_0\left(1+g_D+\phi g\right)^t.\]

Every scenario therefore gets diffusion. Faster frontier progress additionally makes the service more useful and shifts final demand outward.

From usage to compute

Final usage creates inference demand for compute. \(\boldsymbol{K_t^D}\) is total compute demand and \(\boldsymbol{T_t}\) is frontier-training compute demand:

\[K_t^D = \kappa_t Q_t + T_t(g),\]
\[T_t(g)=gT_0.\]

\(\boldsymbol{T_0}\) is frontier-training demand at the reference frontier pace. Slower frontier progress can therefore increase inference usage through lower prices while simultaneously reducing frontier-training demand.

Compute-market equilibrium

Now close the compute market. \(\boldsymbol{S_t}\) is effective compute capacity. The equilibrium compute price is determined by derived compute demand relative to available capacity:

\[p_{K,t}=\mathcal{P}\!\left(\frac{K_t^D}{S_t}\right), \qquad \mathcal{P}' > 0.\]

The applet uses a constant-elasticity inverse-supply schedule for \(\mathcal{P}\). \(\boldsymbol{v}\) is the variable-cost floor and \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) controls short-run supply responsiveness.

Because the compute price \(p_{K,t}\) is itself part of the final AI price \(P_t\), the year's equilibrium is a fixed point:

\[p_{K,t} \rightarrow P_t \rightarrow Q_t \rightarrow K_t^D \rightarrow p_{K,t}.\]

How the equilibrium is computed: the applet begins each year with \(S_t\) and the scenario parameters fixed, guesses \(p_{K,t}\), and then computes the implied final AI price \(P_t\), usage \(Q_t\), and total compute demand \(K_t^D\). That demand implies a new compute price through the inverse-supply schedule. The applet feeds that price back through the same chain and repeats until the compute price stops moving. The converged price and quantity are that year's partial-equilibrium outcome.

Cash flow and investment

Once the equilibrium is found, \(\boldsymbol{H_t}\) is compute-supplier revenue and \(\boldsymbol{CF_t}\) is operating cash flow:

\[H_t = p_{K,t}K_t^D,\]
\[CF_t = \left(p_{K,t}-v\right)K_t^D.\]

This is why final usage does not mechanically determine hyperscaler revenue. Usage can rise while supplier revenue falls if the compute price falls enough; it can also rise while supplier revenue rises if the extra demand keeps compute scarce.

Investment depends on the expected value of adding capacity. \(\boldsymbol{NPV_t}\) is the net present value of one additional unit of effective capacity, \(\boldsymbol{r}\) is the discount rate, \(\boldsymbol{L}\) is the economic life of capacity, \(\boldsymbol{A(r,L)}\) is the corresponding annuity factor, and \(\boldsymbol{I_0}\) is normalized build cost:

\[NPV_t = \frac{CF_t}{S_t}A(r,L)-I_0.\]

\(\boldsymbol{I_t}\) is new investment. It rises with NPV, while effective capacity evolves with investment and retirement:

\[I_t = I(NPV_t), \qquad I' > 0,\]
\[S_{t+1}=(1-\delta)S_t+I_t.\]

\(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) is retirement and obsolescence of effective capacity. In the simulation, investment adjusts gradually toward the rate implied by NPV rather than jumping there instantly.

Investment includes replacement of retiring capacity. For an \(n\)-year horizon, cumulative investment covers the first \(n\) annual intervals. The chart also shows the flow at year \(n\), which starts the following year and is excluded from that total. Usage and revenue comparisons use the endpoint \(t=n\).

Frontier pace

Finally, frontier pace determines how quickly the frontier markup is renewed or competed away. \(\boldsymbol{h}\) is the catch-up half-life:

\[\mu_{F,t}(g)=\mu_{F,0}\left[g+(1-g)2^{-t/h}\right].\]

At the reference pace, frontier differentiation is continually renewed. Under a full pause, the existing markup decays as competitors catch up. Intermediate pacing lies between those cases.

The comparison is therefore one equilibrium tradeoff. Slower frontier progress creates more price pressure through faster markup erosion, which can increase usage, but it also reduces frontier-driven demand growth and frontier-training compute. Those forces determine the equilibrium compute price and quantity, which determine supplier cash flow, NPV, and ultimately investment.
